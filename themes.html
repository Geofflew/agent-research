What we now think

Over the next ten years, agents will take over most of the work of preparing the close. But accuracy will not decide what the product looks like. Three outside forces will. The first is people. Reviewers follow the agent's own signal of how confident it is, so ordinary review stops catching anything the agent missed. The honest error rate has to come from a person who prepares a small sample without seeing the agent's answer. That sample also becomes the one place a junior still learns the work. The second force is clocks that belong to someone else. The model provider retires models on its own schedule. The auditor works on a two-year cycle. The tax authority sets deadlines and default answers, and the lender sets reporting dates. Where those clocks are stricter than the company's own, they decide where the agent may act, where it must stop, and when the month ends.

The third force is outside records. When a bank, a tax authority or a customer records an act, that act becomes final. So the agent needs a hold in front of it. When an outside register checks an act, the agent can be trusted with more. When the authority already holds the data, the company stops preparing it and checks its books against the authority's copy instead. Together these forces change the controller's job. The controller stops approving entries and starts approving the rules and model versions that agents run on. The controller also sets the default answers agents give when nobody replies. The buyer shifts toward the CFO, the lender and the accounting firm. The audit is rebuilt around one agent checking another. The first cut in audit fees comes years late. For a while, the money saved on preparers goes into proving the agents right.

1. Reviewers wave through whatever the agent says it is sure about

The idea. When an agent marks an item as confident, the reviewer waves it through. When it marks an item as uncertain, the reviewer steps in. So review only catches the errors the agent has already flagged. The record of drafts and edits shows how often people agree with the agent, not how often the agent is right. The real error rate comes from a blind sample. In a blind sample, a person prepares a few items from the source documents without seeing the agent's answer first. Most of those items should be ones the agent was sure about. Pricing follows the same pattern. Bookkeeping vendors already charge for transactions nobody touched. That rewards skipping the very review an auditor tests. Close vendors that sell to audited companies will charge instead for each task a reviewer accepts. Acceptance then becomes both the bill and the evidence the auditor looks at. So the contract refunds a task when the reviewer rejects it or the period has to be reopened. The invoice becomes the error-rate report the audit committee reads.

Why we think so. The only field study of professional accountants working with AI covered more than 200,000 transactions at 79 small companies, and it found exactly this pattern. Close vendors already show the confidence mark on the sign-off screen.

What it would change. Each month the preparer gets a short list of items the agent was confident about. Over a year that comes to about sixty items for each type of task. The preparer works each one from the source documents and only then sees the agent's answer. The CFO becomes the buyer, because paying per accepted task is a budget decision. The CFO also keeps the blind sample out of the vendor's billing. By 2031 an audited mid-market company spends most of what it saves on preparers all over again. The money goes to blind samples, extra review and audit hours, and the vendor's fees. Its close costs about what it did in 2026.

The far view. By 2031 the close vendor's error rate for each type of task will be attested in a controls report on the vendor. Payroll processors carry the same kind of report today. The customer's blind sample becomes the one control the customer must still run itself. A competing picture of 2036 has the audit committee running its own agent. The external auditor would then test the board's agent rather than the vendor's.

2. Repeated approvals turn into rules the auditor tests once

The idea. Routine work such as matching, amortization and recurring accruals freezes into a fixed rule. The auditor tests that rule once and then relies on the company's controls over changes to it. So close agents will be built to write rules that a controller approves, not to decide each item. Exceptions that need judgement spread a different way. The agent either posts each item or sends it to a person, depending on how confident it is. Autonomous medical coding spread the same way. What limits this is the quality of the data coming in from the subledgers, more than the model. The threshold is set in dollars as well as in confidence. The dollar line sits against the amount the auditor treats as too small to matter. That threshold becomes the control the auditor tests. One route will not survive. In it, a reviewer approves one treatment and a model quietly decides which later items are similar enough to follow it. Instead the reviewer checks which clause of the company's written policy the agent cited, and which version of it. Whoever holds that library of policy clauses controls the boundary between the ledger and the close platform.

Why we think so. The US auditing standard for internal controls treats the tables and settings an automated control depends on as part of the control. That is what lets a frozen rule be tested once and carried forward.

What it would change. The controller stops approving items. Instead the controller approves changes to a small set of rules and thresholds, each with a version number. Each switch to automatic posting gets its own approval and a fresh sample. So does each threshold change and each rule edit. When a matching rule is edited, the preparer who already signed off reopens the matches that moved. The first reconciliations to go unreviewed are balance-sheet accounts the company's policy already treats as static. The agent works through that list faster, but it does not make the list longer.

The far view. By 2036 standard-setters may publish new accounting rules as rule sets that software can run, with test cases attached. An effective date then becomes a software release that changes every company's frozen rules on one day. Two pictures push the other way. In one, state accountancy laws reserve a short list of decisions to a licensed person the company employs. Going concern and impairment would be on that list, so the law draws the edge of the agent's work. In the other, estimates stay with people because auditing an agent's estimate costs more than preparing it. The audited close ends up half automated and the unaudited close fully automated.

3. AI models get retired faster than audits can trust them

The idea. The AI model inside a close agent belongs to the model provider, not to the company. The provider retires old versions on its own schedule, usually every twelve to sixteen months. Each retirement changes an automated control at every customer in the same week. An auditor only relies on an automated control after it has run unchanged for a full year with a clean result. So the two clocks do not match. The result is a split. Where the model makes a judgement on each item, the vendor will have to freeze one model version for a whole fiscal year, often by hosting the model itself. Where the model only writes a rule that then runs on its own, the rule is what the auditor tests, and the model can change freely.

Why we think so. Providers give between sixty days and six months of notice before retiring a model, and some upgrade customers automatically. One major provider retires a widely used model on 14 January 2027, in the middle of year-end audit work.

What it would change. Choosing a vendor becomes a sorting exercise. The controller decides, control by control, which ones need a frozen model and which can run as rules. When a frozen model does change, the preparers work a blind sample before and after the switch and the controller signs the change off like any other system change. One major cloud service withdraws an old model after fifteen days without use. So the systems accountant keeps the quarter-end model running between closes. The audit fee saving arrives late. The CFO should not count on it before the third audited year.

The far view. By 2031 a model provider might own the consulting arm of a big accounting firm and keep its clients' models frozen through each audit year. Or providers might sell a finance tier that freezes one version for three years with its own controls report, so companies pay for stability rather than for the newest model. A bad model update that misstates the same accrual at hundreds of companies in 2030 would force everyone to freeze. An outage that stops thousands of year-end closes in 2032 would bring back a yearly drill of closing without agents.

4. The auditor's agent will check the company's agent

The idea. Testing every item instead of a sample has been promised since continuous auditing began in 1989. It finally arrives, but as the controller's own monitoring inside the close platform. Auditors have two usual ways to rely on a control, and both fail for an agent. Testing one instance assumes the control acts the same way every time, and an agent does not. Reviewing each output makes the reviewer follow the agent. So at first the auditor discounts the agent's work and tests more, which raises audit cost. Then the auditor runs its own agent over every item. It cuts the fee only after two audited years on an unchanged model. Two wrong models tend to agree with each other, even when they come from different providers. So the auditor still samples the items where both agents agree. Requiring the two agents to use different model families would buy nothing.

Why we think so. The US audit regulator's 2024 amendments accept tests of every item, but they require the auditor to investigate every item a test flags. One large firm's platform already reads more than 1.4 trillion journal lines a year.

What it would change. Fieldwork is the stretch of weeks when the auditors test the books. Before it starts, the controller clears the agent's flagged exceptions every week, because the auditor will chase every one that is left. During fieldwork the controller matches the company's list of exceptions against the auditor's list. The first agent finance staff answer to is the auditor's. Its screening agent sends about one upload in ten straight back to the preparer. Audit evidence needs a positive answer from the company. So a company agent that waits for its reviewer becomes the bottleneck of the audit. At planning the auditor asks which model ran in each month. Only the company's own log can answer that.

The far view. By 2031 one picture has auditors redoing the company's whole routine close blind with their own agents. Each audit then comes down to a few hundred disagreements between two agents. In another, the large firms sell their audit agents as close software to companies they do not audit. In a third, the EU adds agents that prepare listed companies' statements to the AI Act's list of high-risk systems. The first outside check would then be a formal assessment of the vendor, not an audit of the company. By 2036 one picture has the audit committee owning its own ledger agent, with the auditor testing that agent. An international standard pushes against this. It limits how far the auditor may rely on internal audit for testing journal entries.

5. The first limits on agents have nothing to do with accuracy

The idea. The ERP vendor decides which actions an outside agent may take in the ledger. That is a commercial decision. No law opens a ledger the way European payment law opened bank accounts to outside apps. The second limit is payment fraud. The agent reads the invoices and emails that carry fraud attempts, and it cannot reliably ignore instructions planted in them. So changing bank details and releasing payments are taken out of its role. How well that holds depends on how the ledger stores bank details. Some ledgers keep bank accounts behind their own approval step. Others keep them inside the supplier record the agent is allowed to edit. Both limits would hold even for an agent that never made a mistake. So they arrive before any error rate could earn the agent more authority.

Why we think so. The ERP connectors for agents already on the market range from posting journals and payments to read-only access. Crime insurance pays for a loss where a fraudster tricks staff into paying only if an employee called the supplier back and logged the call.

What it would change. A CFO choosing a ledger reads its list of agent actions as a contract term. Once the vendor ships its own agent, it can narrow outside access or start charging for it. The payables clerk keeps making the callback on every changed bank account. Timing follows the ERP replacement cycle. Large SAP customers adopt close agents when they migrate, between 2027 and 2033. The standalone close platform survives as the layer that holds policy and limits across several ERPs.

The far view. By 2031 one picture has ERP vendors charging for every write an outside agent makes. That makes it too expensive for a general-purpose agent to close the books. The rival picture has exactly that agent doing most of the close at small and mid-sized companies. Close vendors shrink to publishing procedure skills in the model provider's marketplace. In Europe, the Data Act may be read to force those writes open. Other pictures move payment control off the ERP altogether. In one, a 2029 fraud campaign plants instructions in supplier invoices. Afterwards banks pay agents only to accounts the bank has verified. In another, card networks issue agent tokens that set what a purchasing agent may buy. In a third, the crime insurer decides whether a payables agent may act on a changed account. By 2036 the close platform may own the controls for every agent that touches money. The ERP would become an interchangeable engine for posting entries. The exception is a ledger that runs its own agents so continuously that month end is just a lock on books already reconciled.

6. Once an outsider records an act, the agent cannot take it back

The idea. An act becomes expensive to undo the moment a party outside the company records it. A journal in an open period can be reversed by the company itself. So the ordinary off switch is the period status the controller already sets. The hard limits belong at three points. The first is releasing a payment. The second is locking a period so nothing more can post to it. The third is issuing an invoice in countries where invoices clear through the tax authority. Clearance means the authority receives and checks each invoice before the customer gets it. Where a public register checks an act, the limit can loosen. So agents will first add new supplier bank accounts in countries where the authority publishes which accounts belong to which tax number. Where the authority and the banks already hold the data, the company's job changes. The close platform's outward job becomes checking the ledger against their records. The first outside agents to act on a company's numbers will read the authority's copy and write nothing. The hold before an act is a choice, not a law of nature. In Italy every business invoice has cleared through the authority since 2019, and reviewing before sending is only suggested practice. France's first invoicing platforms send automatically, with no window to correct.

Why we think so. From September 2026 in France, an issued e-invoice can be corrected only by a new invoice that both the customer and the authority see. Poland publishes every pair of tax number and bank account daily, and a buyer who pays an unlisted account becomes liable for the supplier's tax.

What it would change. A French billing clerk starts the day with invoices the agent has drafted and held for approval. The controller decides which types of invoice the agent may send alone. A Polish payables agent adds new supplier accounts itself once the register matches. The same agent in Germany waits for a person to call the supplier back. In India, public-sector banks lend to small businesses based on their tax returns and bank data. There the close stops being an input to the loan. The accountant's month ends when the agent's list of invoices that differ from the return is cleared. That has to happen before the return locks on the 20th. In Italy only intermediaries such as the company's accountant may hold access to the authority's copy of its invoices. So closing the books comes down to granting the lender that access.

The far view. By 2031 one picture has mid-market lenders in clearance countries reading a borrower's sales, purchases and cash straight from the authority and the banks. The borrower's close becomes a queue of disputes with the outside record. A harder version has the authority drafting small companies' books itself. Another picture has the French tax authority become the first body to certify a finance agent, through the way it accredits invoicing platforms. By 2036 the pictures pull apart. In one, the bank that holds the operating account keeps the small company's books outright. In another, two companies' agents agree each sale on one cleared record that both ledgers post. In a third, the payment date on each cleared invoice is the one term left to negotiate. The two agents price it at clearance.

7. Lender-backed private companies will be first to review only exceptions

The idea. Almost two-thirds of medium and larger private companies have no audit. Their outside check is a review for the lender, often prepared by an accounting firm on a fixed fee. That firm keeps every saving its agents produce. So it will be the first to review only the exceptions. Independence rules limit what an outside accountant may do for a client it also reports on. Under those rules, the owner's monthly acceptance of the agent's decisions becomes the control the lender's review rests on. That sign-off protects the firm only for bookkeeping. A firm that designs and runs the agent's rules as an ongoing service is running the client's information system. No sign-off fixes that. This buyer wants a reliable package for the lender, not a faster close. Covenants are the financial limits a loan sets. People spend their attention on the adjustments to earnings, because that is where the room under the covenants lives. A lender that can log into the books still keeps the audit. The audit tests the accruals and estimates, and a live view shows them but does not check them.

Why we think so. In 2018, 53 percent of outsourced accounting practices billed mainly by the hour, and now only 10 percent do. One loan agreement filed in 2023 demands live logins to the borrower's systems and audited statements in the same document.

What it would change. The firm's staff work a queue of agent entries that wait, unposted, for the owner's monthly sitting. The reviewer spends their hours on the schedule of earnings add-backs. Add-backs are the one-off costs a borrower adds back to show higher earnings. Each line cites the loan clause that allows it. The lender's analyst covers 20 to 25 borrowers a month. With the clauses cited, the analyst checks them instead of asking for detail. The controller at a borrower whose close is run by agents keeps paying for the audit. That controller also answers the analyst whenever the live feed shows something before the monthly package does.

The far view. By 2031 one picture has the accounting firm running the close for hundreds of small clients. It prices each client by how many exceptions its agents raise. Companies with messy subledgers get repriced or dropped. There are several rival pictures. A private equity sponsor buys one close for forty portfolio companies. It sells the close with them at exit, along with five years of error rates. Direct lenders bundle the borrower's agent-run close into the loan. Regulators separate agent-close firms from audit firms by ownership after a platform owned by private equity fails. Standard-setters demand more detailed reporting once agents make detail cheap. By 2036 the pictures disagree about the audit. In one, banks drop the audit in exchange for access to the ledger and a measured error rate. In others, private lenders price how checkable the books are into the interest rate. Or financial statement insurance replaces the audit. Or the ledger vendor becomes the main lender. We expect the audit to stay. An error rate on entries still does not test the estimates.

8. Companies will stop hiring juniors to prepare the close

The idea. Finance teams will shrink by not hiring juniors, not by layoffs. The pyramid of many juniors under a few managers turns into a diamond. This happens during a shortage that already exists. By about 2032 the hard limit is the supply of mid-level people who can handle exceptions. The seat that goes first is the junior who checks the agent's output line by line. It goes before any study shows the agent is reliable. The shortage turns an empty seat into a control weakness faster than anyone can run a study. The manager's review of new treatments, estimates and policy stays. Close errors only come to light later, as restatements, so that judgement cannot be dropped. Juniors who only review agent output learn less. So preparing the blind sample by hand becomes both their training and the company's measure of the agent. We dropped the idea that juniors disappear on the audit side too. Large UK firms are taking on more audit trainees, from 720 to 764 at one firm in its 2025 year. We also dropped the comparison with translation, where machine output grew about 47-fold. Close volume is fixed by the number of transactions, so it cannot grow that way.

Why we think so. More than 300,000 US accountants and auditors left their jobs in two years. Only 13 percent of CFOs expect more junior roles.

What it would change. The manager's queue holds only new treatments, estimates and policy citations. It peaks in the third month of each quarter. That is when reconciliation tools hand the sign-off on accounts that passed their rules back to a person. A new licensing route lets graduates qualify as CPAs after two years of supervised work. Its first cohorts finish in Texas from August 2028 and in California from January 2029. Under it, the supervising accountant compares the junior's blind-sample figure with the agent's. The supervisor no longer checks reconciliations the junior built.

The far view. By 2036 one picture has a first-year accountant's whole job being the blind sample. Licensing boards count it as the only supervised experience. Another has first-year accountants hired to attack the company's own finance agents, because insurers and auditors require that testing. The opposing pictures run the other way. Companies that kept no training queue face material weaknesses for not having enough skilled staff. A material weakness is a control failure serious enough to be reported publicly. And companies under about $250 million of revenue rent a licensed controller's signature. One professional signs for twenty or thirty companies.

9. Every point where an agent stops and waits has a clock

The idea. Today's close agents wait for a finance reviewer and ask nobody outside finance. So the preparer does not disappear. The preparer becomes the person who chases receipts, budget owners and unposted invoices. Close schedulers already on the market hold a task until the task before it closes. They report the delay rather than re-plan. No re-plan can produce a missing figure anyway. So agents shorten the close only by booking estimates for late inputs at a cutoff. That moves the error out of this period and into next period's corrections. When agents do ask people, they should ask the way hospitals query doctors. The question carries no proposed figure and offers the answer "I don't know." It should not be a pre-filled estimate to approve. The checklist survives, signed by agents with their output attached. Each agent gets a named owner. That owner signs the quarterly sub-certification for the agent's settings and results. A sub-certification is the internal sign-off that feeds the CFO's own certification. Outside the company the logic flips. Some counterparties and tax authorities treat silence as acceptance. There, an agent that waits has already made a decision. Its limit is a deadline it must escalate before.

Why we think so. In Mexico, a buyer who ignores a supplier's request to cancel an invoice for three business days is treated as accepting it. Chile treats an unchallenged invoice as accepted after eight days, and Colombia after three business days.

What it would change. Each day the Mexican payables clerk works a short queue of cancellation requests the agent did not decide. The clerk matches each replacement invoice before the third day. The controller sets the default answer to rejection for invoices already paid. In Chile the agent passes any unreviewed invoice to a person by about day five of the eight. Inside the company, an agent that signs off its own lines early still waits on the slowest step before it. That step usually belongs to someone outside finance.

The far view. By 2031 one picture has an orchestrator re-planning the close every hour. An orchestrator is an agent that schedules the other agents and the people. It plans around the people outside finance who hold the missing inputs. Managers in sales, procurement and legal then carry close deadlines. The controller manages answers rather than accounting. By 2036 the outside edge dominates. Balance confirmations run from one system to another with no person on either side. One company's agents answer another's, using default answers the controller set in advance.
