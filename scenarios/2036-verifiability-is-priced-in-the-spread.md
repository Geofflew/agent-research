# By 2036 US private credit prices how verifiable a borrower's numbers are, so a mid-market company whose close produces a measured error rate and bank-reconciled revenue borrows at a lower spread, and the close becomes something the CFO sells to the lender rather than a cost

Year: 2036
Status: retired (0003F, cap 8/year: lowest 2036 net (10.07) when 2036-audit-committee-runs-its-own-ledger-agent scored 10.47; the verifiability-priced-in-the-spread mechanism (Ghosh-Vallee-Zeng 42bp data-sharing discount, UK SME data sharing, measured error rate as a pricing input) moved to threads/reporting-frequency-and-lender-clock.)
Contradicts: 2036-bank-keeps-the-small-company-ledger. There the lender keeps the borrower's books and needs no evidence about how they were prepared; here the borrower keeps its own ledger and sells the lender evidence of how often it is wrong, which is only worth buying from a borrower the lender does not already keep books for.
Depends on:
- C19 (agent speed is captured in the lender's package and cost per close, not public reporting): the lender is the outside party that pays for the close's output; if public markets valued it more, the pricing would appear in equity, not loan spreads.
- C12 (a usable error rate comes only from a blind sample): lenders price the blind-sample rate; if an acceptance rate from ordinary review were usable, every borrower would report a near-perfect figure carrying no information.
- C29 (the review concentrates on the add-back schedule, where the slack lives): verified entries leave the add-backs as the one negotiated part; if the slack were in the entries, a verified close would remove it and the spread effect would be much larger.
- C45 (the first exception-only closes are at unaudited private companies whose books a CPA firm keeps on a fixed fee; C27 retired into it in 0007A): most of these borrowers are unaudited, so the lender has a reason to pay for another kind of evidence; if they were audited, the audit would carry the assurance and the error rate would add little.
Clocks: direct lenders' five-year loans written in 2026 refinance in 2031 and those written in 2031 refinance in 2036, so two refinancing vintages separate today's covenants from the scenario; the national insurance regulator is building a process, with system work expected to begin in 2026, that lets its securities office challenge private letter ratings, and insurers hold a large share of private credit, so rating agencies have to show what their private ratings rest on; lenders' quarterly compliance certificates arrive forty-five days after quarter end, which sets the cadence at which the error rate is reported.
Yield: 0.0

In 2036 a US distributor with $150 million of revenue refinances its direct-lender loan and gets two quotes that differ by a margin larger than the difference between a strong and a weak leverage ratio. The cheaper lender requires a reporting covenant the company can already meet: monthly read access to the ledger and bank feeds, revenue reconciled to bank receipts by the company's agents, and the blind-sample error rate for each close task class, prepared by its accounting firm and reported with each compliance certificate. The dearer quote accepts the package without that evidence. The private rating agency that rates the loan for the insurance company buying it scores reporting verifiability as a separate factor, because the insurers' regulator now challenges private ratings it disagrees with. What the CFO sells the lender is a measured process, and the close budget is justified by the spread it saves.

Mechanism: Lenders to small and mid-sized companies already take direct read access to borrowers' accounting systems through data-connection services, and direct lenders test maintenance covenants monthly or quarterly (C29). Once agents prepare most of the close and borrowers' accounting firms run blind samples (C12, C45), a lender reading the ledger can also read how often the ledger is wrong, class by class. Verifiable cash-flow data already changes credit terms: on Indian small-business loans, borrowers whose sales ran more through verifiable payments paid about 42 basis points less for an interquartile difference and defaulted less, and after the UK made banks share small firms' account data those firms were 26 percent more likely to form a new lending relationship, so a lender that can verify a borrower's whole ledger can price it lower. As the insurers' regulator's challenge process comes online after 2026, rating agencies must justify private ratings, and verifiability gives them something to cite. The 2031 refinancing vintage writes the error-rate covenant into some loans; by the 2036 vintage the spread gap is wide enough that borrowers without a measured close pay for it.

The CFO stops treating the close as overhead and decides whether a lower error rate in revenue and payables is worth the extra blind samples. The accounting firm sells the error-rate report as a separate product and signs it each quarter. The credit officer stops reading the package line by line, watches the error-rate trend and the add-backs, and reprices at the next amendment if the rate rises. The rating analyst adds a verifiability score to the loan's file.

What would settle it: a credit agreement requiring an error rate or ledger access; lender data showing lower default rates for borrowers with direct ledger feeds; a rating methodology that scores reporting quality for private loans.

Signposts: a direct lender advertising lower pricing for borrowers granting ledger and bank access before the 2031 vintage; failing signpost, by the 2031 vintage lenders still price only on leverage and coverage, and ledger access stays a monitoring convenience with no price.
