# By 2031 direct lenders pay for their mid-market borrowers' agent close and bundle it into the loan, because a clean monthly feed is worth more to the lender than the software costs, so the buyer of the close in that segment is the lender rather than the borrower's CFO

Year: 2031
Status: retired (0001F, review 0004 cap: lender cluster merge (review 0004); the lender-as-buyer mechanism moved to threads/reporting-frequency-and-lender-clock.)
Depends on:
- C19 (the value of agent speed is captured in the lender's monthly package and in cost per close, not in public reporting): the lender is the party that gains from a reliable monthly package, which is why it is willing to pay; if public reporting valued the close more, the borrower would keep buying it for its own reasons.
- C29 (in the lender-driven close the review concentrates on the covenant add-back schedule): the lender's monitoring rests on a clean feed for entries and a person on the add-backs, which is the product it pays for; if the slack sat in managed entries, a bundled close would not reduce the lender's monitoring cost.
- C27 (the first exception-only closes are at unaudited private companies, run by accounting firms on fixed fees): the bundled close is delivered by such a firm, and the borrower's accounting firm becomes a subcontractor of the lender's platform; if firms did not run agent closes, the lender would have no one to deliver it.
- C34 (the firm whose agents post a client's entries cannot also give the lender the review report): the lender paying for the close cannot also take the firm's review as independent, so the lender relies on its own monitoring and a separate firm's review where it needs one; if the paying relationship did not matter, the lender could use one firm for both.
Clocks: a typical direct-lender loan asks for monthly statements in about thirty days and a compliance certificate in about forty-five; loans written in 2026 refinance in 2031, the first vintage that can write the bundled close into the credit agreement; firms moved from 53 percent hourly billing in 2018 to 10 percent by 2024, so a lender can buy a fixed-fee close per borrower.
Yield: 0.0

In 2031 a direct lender offers its mid-market borrowers a term sheet with a line that would have looked odd in 2026: the lender pays for the borrower's close. It contracts with a network of accounting firms that run agent closes on fixed fees, connects them to the borrower's ledger and banks, and receives the monthly package, the add-back schedule, and a feed of the ledger on the thirtieth day of every month. The borrower's CFO no longer buys close software or a bookkeeping service; the cost is built into the loan's margin. For a borrower with weak finance staff the offer is attractive, because the close arrives cleaner and on time; for the lender it is cheaper than chasing late packages, re-keying them, and paying analysts to find errors. The accounting firms that run these closes now sell mostly to lenders, not to companies, and the close software vendors' largest customers in the segment are three or four private credit managers.

Mechanism: Direct lenders already depend on monthly packages that arrive late and in inconsistent formats, and late reporting is itself a warning sign under the covenants (C29). Agent closes run by fixed-fee firms (C27) make a standardized monthly package cheap to produce at scale. A lender with hundreds of borrowers finds that paying for one standard close per borrower costs less than its monitoring staff and gives it the same format across the book, which is the value C19 places in the lender package rather than in public reporting. Because the lender pays, it cannot treat the firm's review as independent (C34), so it keeps its own monitoring and, where a review is required, buys it from a second firm. The 2031 refinancing vintage writes the bundled close into new credit agreements.

The borrower's CFO stops buying close software and stops managing a bookkeeping firm, and decides instead whether to accept the lender's close or pay for an independent one to keep a negotiating position. The lender's portfolio manager stops chasing packages and works a queue of exceptions across a standardized book. The accounting firm's partner stops selling to companies and negotiates per-borrower rates with three lenders. The close software vendor's account executive sells to a private credit manager's operations team.

What would settle it: a direct lender offering to pay for borrowers' bookkeeping or close; a credit agreement that names the lender's chosen accounting firm for monthly reporting; a close vendor announcing a contract with a private credit manager covering its portfolio companies or borrowers.

Signposts: private credit managers buying close software for portfolio companies or borrowers before the 2031 refinancing vintage; an accounting firm network forming to serve one lender's borrowers; failing signpost, borrowers refuse lender-paid closes for fear of lock-in, and lenders keep buying only monitoring software for their own staff.
