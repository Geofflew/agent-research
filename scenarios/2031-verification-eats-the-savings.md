# By 2031 an audited mid-market company spends most of what agents saved in preparation hours on verifying the agents, so the close costs about what it cost in 2026 while the money moves from preparers' salaries to samples, reviewers, audit hours, and usage fees

Year: 2031
Status: retired (0001F, review 0004 cap: below the 2031 cap of 8; mechanism moved to threads/acceptance-billing-conflict.)
Depends on:
- C12 (a usable error rate comes only from a blind sample): blind preparation is the first new cost line, about 1,200 to 1,900 items a year for twenty task classes; if ordinary review produced a usable rate, this line would not exist.
- C25 (external auditors discount agent-prepared evidence, raising audit testing and cost): extended audit testing is the second new line; if auditors relied on agent work from the start, audit fees would fall instead.
- C17 (teams shrink by not hiring juniors, and the binding constraint becomes the supply of mid-level reviewers): the reviewers who remain are scarce and paid more, which is the third line; if mid-level reviewers were plentiful, their pay would not rise.
- C28 (close platforms price agents per reviewer-accepted task, with sampled items never billed): usage fees scale with the work the agent does, so the vendor's share of the saving grows with adoption; if agents were priced by seat, the vendor would capture less.
Clocks: the first audits under the US amendments on technology-assisted analysis cover fiscal years beginning on or after 15 December 2025, so the first fee increases on agent-prepared work appear in the calendar 2026 audits and are disclosed in 2027 proxies; companies' audit fees are disclosed every proxy season, so the 2027 to 2031 proxies give five years of comparison; close platform contracts renew on one- to three-year terms, so per-task pricing reaches most customers by the 2028 to 2029 renewals.
Yield: 0.0

In its March 2031 budget review the CFO of a $500 million audited company puts the close cost next to 2026, line by line. In 2026 it was six preparers at about $95,000 loaded ($570,000), two reviewers at $140,000 ($280,000), the close-related share of the audit fee ($600,000), and close software ($80,000), about $1.53 million. In 2031 four preparer seats were never refilled, but two staff accountants spend most of their year preparing the blind sample ($190,000); the two reviewers now cost $185,000 each because people who can judge exceptions are scarce ($370,000); the close-related audit fee rose to $750,000 in the 2027 audit when the auditors extended testing on agent work and has come only partly back, to $680,000; and per-task usage fees, which replaced the $80,000 software license and were repriced at the 2028 and 2029 renewals, are $260,000. The total is about $1.50 million. What changed is not the amount but what it buys: a measured error rate per class, a steadier lender package, and a team that no longer depends on preparers who can quit before year end. The benchmark firms that used to rank finance teams by headcount per billion of revenue start ranking them by cost per verified task class, and the board's question changes from how many people finance needs to what error rate it is paying for.

Mechanism: Agents take preparation first, and companies stop hiring juniors (C17). The error rate the auditor and the controller need can come only from blind preparation (C12), which brings back a share of the preparation work in a new form. Auditors discount agent evidence and extend testing (C25), and the fee increase appears from the calendar 2026 audits onward. Fewer juniors means fewer people becoming reviewers, and the first licensees of the new experience route only finish in August 2028 and January 2029, so reviewer pay rises through the period (C17). Per-task pricing (C28) moves the saving toward the vendor as adoption grows, and each renewal resets the price upward. Each cost line is a direct result of the control the thesis says agent work requires, so the more carefully a company adopts, the more of the saving it spends.

The CFO stops presenting agents as a headcount saving and starts presenting them as a reliability purchase, and decides how many classes are worth the cost of a blind sample. The controller stops managing preparers and starts managing a budget split across samplers, reviewers, the audit, and the vendor. The audit committee stops expecting audit fees to fall and asks for the error rates instead. The close platform's sales team stops selling hours saved and sells per-class reliability.

What would settle it: a company's own accounting of close cost before and after agent adoption, including blind sampling and audit fees; proxy disclosures of audit fee changes attributed to agent-prepared work from 2027; reviewer salary surveys showing mid-level pay rising faster than other finance roles.

Signposts: audit fee increases attributed to agent-prepared work in the 2027 proxy season; per-task close platform invoices rising at the 2028 and 2029 renewals; failing signpost, a public company reporting by 2030 that close cost fell by more than half after agents including sampling and audit, which would mean verification is cheap.
