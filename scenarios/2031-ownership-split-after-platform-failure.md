In 2031 the most argued question in the accounting profession is who may own both kinds of firm. A private-equity-owned platform has two halves under one parent: a consulting company that runs agent-prepared closes for 4,000 mid-market private clients on fixed fees, and a licensed attest firm, majority-owned by its partners, that audits or reviews some of the same clients. In 2030 a manufacturing client's agent-run close misclassified a growing customer prepayment as revenue for six quarters; the consulting company's reviewers saw only exceptions and accepted 99 percent (illustrative) of agent output; the attest firm's review engagement relied on that acceptance rate and on analytics that the same agents prepared. The client breached its covenants when the revenue reversed, and the lender sued. The case showed that the independence line drawn between legal entities did not hold when both entities' incentives ran to the same owner's sale price. By the end of 2031 the profession's ethics body and the states treat common ownership of an agent-close business and an attest firm as an impairment for shared clients, and the platforms sell or spin off one half before their exits.

Mechanism: Private equity bought into large accounting firms from 2021 to 2024 by splitting each into an attest firm owned by licensed partners and a consulting company the investors own, an arrangement the profession's rules already permit. The consulting halves run agent closes on fixed fees with exception-only review (C45), because automation margin drives the exit price. Their reviewers measure acceptance rather than a blind error rate, and acceptance runs near 100 percent. The attest halves, unlike auditors generally (C25), have commercial reasons not to discount their sister company's output. C45's independence condition is met because the agents sit in the other entity, so nobody checks the common ownership until a covenant failure brings in a lender's lawyers, and the 2030 peer review cycle and the lawsuit put the case in front of the states and the ethics body in the same year.

The platform's chief executive stops selling the combined offering and decides which half to sell first. The attest partner stops relying on the sister company's acceptance data and starts asking clients for a blind sample drawn by someone outside the platform. The lender's credit officer starts asking, in every new deal, who owns the firm that prepares the close and who owns the firm that reviews it. The client's CFO, who chose the platform because one vendor did everything, has to hire a second firm for the review.

What would settle it: the ownership structure and shared-client counts of the large private-equity-backed firms; any ethics inquiry or state board action on common ownership of a consulting company and an attest firm; a lender's due-diligence questionnaire asking who owns the preparer and the reviewer.

Signposts: the first sale or recapitalization of a private-equity-backed accounting firm between 2028 and 2031, and whether the buyer takes both halves; an ethics body project on alternative practice structures and agent services before the 2030 peer review cycle; failing signpost, platforms exit with both halves intact and no regulator treats common ownership as an issue through 2032.
