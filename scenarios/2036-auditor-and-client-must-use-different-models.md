In 2036 an audit engagement letter names two things it did not name in 2026: the model family behind the client's close agents and the model family behind the audit firm's agents, and they must differ. The rule followed a 2033 inspection finding. One large firm's agent, re-preparing revenue blind, agreed with clients' agents on bill-and-hold sales that both misread the same way, because both ran on one provider's models. Dozens of clients restated. Regulators drew aviation's lesson for flight computers: independent checks must be dissimilar, or they fail together. By 2036 audit firms keep agents on at least two model families and assign them against each client's choice, and a close vendor's choice of model provider narrows which audit firms can easily take its customers.

Mechanism: Close vendors and audit firms build agents on a few model providers. Auditors run their own agents over every entry (C46) and re-prepare blind (C12), which looks like independence. But two models from the same family trained on similar data share systematic errors, so a blind re-preparation by a sibling model confirms the client's mistake. The first time this produces restatements at many clients of one firm, the annual inspection finds it within a cycle, the firm's own quality control review under the new standard has to explain it, and the regulator proposes dissimilarity as a condition of using an agent as audit evidence. Frozen rules regain share (C14), and each provider retirement now forces both parties to re-pair (C38).

The audit partner stops choosing tools by price and capability alone and starts checking each client's model family before assigning the engagement's agents. The client's controller starts asking the close vendor which model provider it uses, because a switch can force the auditor to switch too. The regulator's inspectors start sampling engagements by model pairing.

What would settle it: a documented case of two same-family agents agreeing on the same wrong answer in accounting work; an audit firm disclosing its agents' model families; a regulator discussing independence between auditor and client tools.

Signposts: inspection findings on tool independence after the first agent-heavy audits; an audit firm announcing agents on more than one model provider for independence reasons by 2030; failing signpost, through 2033 no correlated miss is reported and firms standardize on one model provider, which would mean shared blind spots are rare in practice.
