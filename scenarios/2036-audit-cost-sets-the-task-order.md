# By 2036 audited companies have handed agents only the close work an auditor can test cheaply, while estimates and judgments stay with people because auditing an agent's estimate costs more than preparing it, so the audited close is half automated and the unaudited close is fully automated

Year: 2036
Status: retired (0001F, review 0004 cap: lowest net in 2036 under the cap of 8; mechanism moved to threads/which-close-tasks-go-first.)
Depends on:
- C25 (external auditors discount agent-prepared evidence, so agent work first raises audit testing and cost): this scenario says the discount never fully lifts for estimates; if auditors come to accept agent estimates as readily as management's, the cost barrier falls and estimates move too.
- C14 (the first reliance comes through the existing route for automated controls, only for work frozen into a deterministic, change-controlled rule): the work that moves is exactly the work that can be frozen into a rule; if auditors found a route for item-by-item model judgment, the line between the two halves would disappear.
- C27 (the first exception-only closes are at unaudited private companies, where the outside check is a lender's review engagement): the unaudited half of the scenario rests on this; if lenders demanded audit-level evidence, unaudited closes would face the same barrier.
- C26 (every switch of a class to auto-post or change of a threshold is a configuration change that restarts the evidence): estimate classes change assumptions every period, so their evidence never accumulates; if changes did not restart evidence, estimates could earn reliance over time.
Clocks: the US auditing standard on accounting estimates and the amendments on using the work of specialists apply to audits of fiscal years ending on or after 15 December 2020, and the amendments on technology-assisted analysis to fiscal years beginning on or after 15 December 2025; the revised international audit evidence standard governs audits from mid-2029, so by 2036 auditors have about five cycles of experience under it; the first two-year licensing cohorts reach manager level between 2035 and 2037.
Yield: 0.0

In 2036 a public company's close is split down the middle. Reconciliations, matching, amortization, depreciation, intercompany eliminations, and recurring accruals run on agent-authored rules that the auditor tests once and then controls through change management, and nobody prepares them. Reserves, impairment tests, revenue judgments on new contracts, and other estimates are still prepared by people, with agents drafting supporting analysis that the preparer reworks, because when an agent prepares an estimate the auditor treats the model like a management specialist: it tests the model's method, its data, and its assumptions, and re-performs the estimate, and that work costs more than the hours the agent saves. The private company down the street, unaudited and lender-financed, has no such split: its accounting firm runs the whole close on agents, estimates included, and the lender's review engagement accepts it. Which close tasks moved to agents, and in what order, was decided by what an auditor could test cheaply, not by which procedures were clearest or which data was easiest to reach.

Mechanism: Today auditors discount evidence that comes from an AI system (C25), and the estimates standard and the specialist amendments already require them to evaluate the method, data, and assumptions behind any estimate management did not make by hand. In the 2027 and 2028 audits, agent-prepared estimates draw extended testing and higher fees, while frozen rules draw a one-time test (C14). Estimate classes change assumptions every period, so they never accumulate the stable history that would let reliance build (C26), and the fee gap persists through the international standard's first cycles after 2029. CFOs, who see audit fees and close cost on the same budget line, keep people on estimates and move everything rule-shaped to agents. Unaudited companies, checked by a lender's review of inquiry and analytics (C27), face no such cost and automate estimates too, so by 2036 the two markets have different closes.

The controller of an audited company stops treating automation as a single program and decides class by class whether the audit cost of agent preparation exceeds the saving, and keeps a small team of estimate preparers who are now the most senior accountants below them. The external auditor stops sampling rule-based classes and spends most of the engagement on estimates, re-performing agent-drafted analyses where the company used them. The accounting firm running a private company's close stops distinguishing estimates from routine work and prices the close as one fixed fee. The audit committee asks for an annual chart of which classes are agent-run and the audit hours each one draws.

What would settle it: audit fee data that separates hours on agent-prepared estimates from hours on rule-based classes; any audit firm methodology treating an agent-prepared estimate as the work of a management specialist; a close vendor reporting that its audited customers automate estimates later than its unaudited customers.

Signposts: audit firms publishing guidance on agent-prepared estimates under the specialist amendments before the international standard takes effect in mid-2029; audited companies' disclosures of audit fee increases tied to estimates in the 2027 and 2028 proxy cycles; failing signpost, by the 2031 audits agent-prepared estimates are tested no more heavily than management's own, which would let estimates move and erase the split.
