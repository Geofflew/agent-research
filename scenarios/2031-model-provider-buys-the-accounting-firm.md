# By 2031 a model provider owns the consulting half of a large US accounting firm, bought from its private equity owners at exit, so the agents that keep thousands of small companies' books and the model they run on belong to one company, and the partners keep only the attest half

Year: 2031
Status: live
Contradicts: 2031-sponsor-runs-one-close-for-the-portfolio. There private equity keeps outsourced closes as a service it controls and sells to its own portfolio; here the investors who own accounting firms' advisory halves sell them to a model provider at exit, so the close capacity leaves private equity and sponsors become the provider's customers.
Depends on:
- C45 (a firm whose agents post a client's entries can give the lender a review only while the owner accepts the agent's decisions, because posting is a management responsibility): this is why only the consulting half can be sold to the provider and the attest half stays with partners; if posting did not end independence, the provider could buy the whole firm and sign its own clients' reviews.
- C38 (the model in a close agent changes on the provider's retirement schedule, so each retirement is a program change at every customer at once): a provider that owns the preparer can hold its own clients on one model for their fiscal year while moving everyone else on its schedule, which is worth more to it than to any investor; if vendors controlled the model clock, owning the preparer would buy nothing.
Clocks: private equity entered large US accounting firms through the alternative practice structure from 2021 to 2024, splitting each into a licensed attest firm and an investor-owned advisory company, as Grant Thornton did in May 2024, and a five-to-seven-year hold puts the first exits between 2028 and 2031; the AICPA ethics committee's proposed interpretations distinguishing investors' significant influence from control over non-attest entities take effect one year after adoption, so the rule a buyer must satisfy is fixed by about 2028.
Yield: 0.0

In 2031 the owner of a $15 million (illustrative) landscaping business gets the books from the company that makes the assistant on their phone. The provider bought the advisory half of a large firm in 2029, when its investors sold, and runs four thousand (illustrative) clients' closes on its own models, with the firm's former staff working exceptions and owner sittings. Next door, the partner-owned attest firm still reviews some of those clients and must show each year that the owner, not the provider, accepted the agent's decisions. Independent firms running closes on the same models now buy from a competitor.

Mechanism: Investors split the firms so they could own the advisory half (C45 keeps attest with partners), and at exit they sell to the highest bidder for margin. A model provider bids highest, because the advisory half gives it thousands of ledgers where it already posts and because owning the preparer lets it pin its own clients' model through each audit year while moving all other customers on its retirement schedule (C38). The ethics committee's control rule, fixed by 2028, tells the buyer exactly how far its influence over the attest half may go.

The provider's head of accounting services schedules model retirements against clients' fiscal years. The attest partner decides whether reviewing books the provider's agents prepared still meets the control rule. The independent firm's managing partner decides whether to keep paying a supplier whose subsidiary competes for its clients.

What would settle it: an AI company acquiring or taking a stake in an accounting firm's advisory business; an alternative-practice-structure exit to a technology buyer.

Signposts: by 2030 a model provider owns an accounting firm's advisory business; failing signpost, the 2028 to 2031 exits all go to other investors or back to partners.
