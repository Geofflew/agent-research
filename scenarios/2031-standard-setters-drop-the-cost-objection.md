# By 2031 standard setters, seeing agents make granular reporting cheap, stop accepting preparers' cost objections and require expense and revenue detail that no 2026 close could produce, so the public close trades speed for depth

Year: 2031
Status: retired (0001F, review 0004 cap: second-lowest net in 2031 under the cap of 8; mechanism moved to threads/which-close-tasks-go-first.)
Depends on:
- C19 (the faster close is not bought for public reporting, because reporting is moving to less frequent filing and the median close finishes weeks inside the deadline): agent capacity freed from speed has nowhere to go in public reporting except depth; if speed were valued, the capacity would be spent on earlier filing instead.
- C14 (agents are built to author rules a controller approves, relied on through the route for automated controls): classifying every entry into the new disclosure categories is rule work that agents author and auditors benchmark once; if classification had to be judged item by item, the detail would stay expensive.
- C26 (every switch to auto-post or change of a threshold is a configuration change that restarts the evidence): each new disclosure category is a change to the classification rules, so standard setters that add categories often impose a re-baselining cost, which is the one cost objection that survives; if changes did not restart evidence, there would be no remaining brake.
Clocks: the FASB's expense disaggregation standard applies to annual periods beginning after 15 December 2026 and interim periods beginning after 15 December 2027, so calendar-year companies first report it in 10-Ks filed in early 2028; IFRS 18 applies from 1 January 2027; the SEC proposed in May 2026 to let companies report semiannually; standard setters run a broad agenda consultation roughly every five years, so the consultation after the first disaggregated filings falls around 2029 to 2030, and a standard it adds lands around 2031.
Yield: 0.0

In 2031 the debate at the standard setters has turned around. In 2023 and 2024 preparers argued that breaking expenses into purchases of inventory, employee compensation, depreciation, and amortization within each income statement line would cost too much, and the board narrowed the requirement because of it. By 2031 companies whose agents classify every entry against frozen rules report that the first disaggregated filings cost little to produce, and investors ask for more: expense detail by segment, revenue by contract type and region at a finer level, and the reconciliation of non-GAAP measures entry by entry. The board's cost-benefit analysis, which for decades rested on preparers' estimates of hours, now rests on the cost of writing and re-proving classification rules, which is small. The public close produces less often, if the semiannual option is adopted, but in much greater depth, and the controller's quarter is spent maintaining classification rules rather than racing a deadline.

Mechanism: The expense disaggregation standard and IFRS 18 force the first large classification exercise in 2027 and 2028. Companies that have agents authoring classification rules (C14) produce the tables at low marginal cost, and those without agents bear the cost the board was warned about. When the board's next agenda consultation asks about further disaggregation around 2029 to 2030, the cost evidence comes from the first group, investor demand is unchanged, and speed is no longer a competing use of the close's capacity because public reporting is not getting faster (C19). The board adds detail, and the only cost objection that holds is the re-baselining of rules each time a category changes (C26), so standards come in larger, less frequent batches.

The controller stops racing a filing deadline and starts owning a library of classification rules, deciding how each new category maps to the chart of accounts and when to freeze the mapping for the auditor. The equity analyst stops estimating cost structures from footnotes and models from disclosed detail. The standard-setting board member stops weighing preparers' hour estimates and starts weighing the re-baselining cost of each new category. The auditor tests the classification rules once and their change log after.

What would settle it: preparers' reported cost of the first expense disaggregation filings in 2028, split by whether agents classified entries; comment letters in the next agenda consultation; a standard setter's basis for conclusions citing automation in its cost analysis.

Signposts: comment letters on the 2029 to 2030 agenda consultation in which preparers stop citing cost against further disaggregation; a basis for conclusions that cites automated classification; failing signpost, the first disaggregated 10-Ks in 2028 report high cost and boards narrow future requirements, which would mean agents did not lower the cost of depth.
