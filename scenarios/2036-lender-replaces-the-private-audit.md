# By 2036 direct lenders drop the audited-annual requirement for mid-market borrowers that give them continuous ledger access and a blind-sample error rate, so the private-company audit shrinks to companies preparing to sell or list

Year: 2036
Status: retired (0001F, review 0004 cap: lender cluster merge (review 0004), overlaps 2036-verifiability-is-priced-in-the-spread; mechanism moved to threads/reporting-frequency-and-lender-clock.)
Depends on:
- C27 (the first exception-only closes are at unaudited private companies, where the outside check is a lender's review engagement of inquiry and analytics): the lender is already the outside party that matters for these companies and already accepts less than an audit from many of them; if lenders cared nothing about how the close was prepared, they would have no reason to swap the audit for anything.
- C12 (a usable error rate comes only from a sample prepared blind): the lender's substitute for the audit is the blind-sample rate; if ordinary review produced a usable rate, every borrower would report a perfect one and the lender could not rely on it.
- C29 (in the lender-driven close the review concentrates on the covenant add-back schedule, where the slack lives): the lender keeps a person on the add-backs and lets the feed cover the entries; if the slack were in the entries, the lender would need an auditor's transaction testing after all.
- C34 (the firm whose agents post a client's entries cannot also give the lender the review report): the accounting firm that runs the close cannot be the one that certifies it, which is why lenders build their own monitoring rather than buy a second firm's assurance; if one firm could do both, the lender would simply accept that firm's report.
Clocks: a typical direct-lender loan today asks for monthly statements in about thirty days, quarterly statements and a compliance certificate in about forty-five, and audited annual statements in ninety to one hundred twenty; five-year loans written in 2026 refinance in 2031 and those written in 2031 in 2036, so two vintages of credit agreements can drop or keep the audit clause; the SBA has required an independent quality-of-earnings report on certain change-of-ownership loans of $3 million or more since 1 October 2026, which shows a lender-driven check can replace or supplement an audit by rule.
Yield: 0.0

In 2036 a direct lender's standard credit agreement for a $100 million revenue borrower no longer requires audited annual statements. In their place it requires read access to the general ledger and bank feeds, the blind-sample error rate for each close task class each quarter, and the add-back schedule tied to the agreement's clauses. The lender's monitoring agent reads the feed weekly, tests revenue against bank receipts and the error rates against agreed tolerances, and flags drift to a credit officer. Borrowers save the audit fee, which for a company this size was often the largest outside finance cost after the bank, and many take the saving. The private-company audit does not disappear, but it shrinks to companies preparing for a sale or listing, companies with many outside shareholders, and those whose lenders still want it; mid-sized accounting firms lose audit work and build monitoring and sampling services instead.

Mechanism: Today about two-thirds of medium-to-larger private companies already produce no audited statements, and lenders tier their requirements by loan size. Direct lenders already read borrowers' numbers monthly against maintenance covenants (C29) and already accept review engagements from many borrowers (C27). As agents prepare the close, borrowers' accounting firms run blind samples that give a measured error rate (C12), and lenders begin taking ledger access as data-connection services make it routine. A lender that reads the feed and the rate every quarter learns more about the borrower than an audit dated three months after year end tells it. The firm that runs the close cannot certify it (C34), so the lender builds monitoring itself rather than buying a second firm's report. In the 2031 vintage some lenders offer to drop the audit clause for borrowers that grant feed access and report the rate; by the 2036 vintage it is the standard offer.

The borrower's CFO stops scheduling an annual audit and starts managing what the lender's agent sees each week, including the error-rate trend that could trigger a review. The lender's credit officer stops waiting for audited statements and works a queue of borrowers whose feed drifted, deciding when to ask for a one-off review or audit. The mid-sized audit partner loses private-company audits and sells blind-sample preparation and data-quality reviews. The private-equity owner preparing a sale commissions the first audit in years, two years before the sale.

What would settle it: a credit agreement that substitutes ledger access or an error-rate report for audited annual statements; lender data comparing loss rates of borrowers monitored by feed with those monitored by audit; audit firms reporting falling private-company audit counts tied to lender changes.

Signposts: a direct lender advertising audit-free terms for borrowers with ledger access, in the 2031 refinancing vintage; regulators or rating agencies that rate private credit commenting on audit-free covenants; failing signpost, lenders keep stepping requirements up from reviewed to audited through 2031, as some have been doing, which would mean they want more third-party assurance, not less.
