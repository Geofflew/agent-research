What we know so far: The Radiology experiment behind the anchoring claims is a lab test of 27 radiologists on 40 mammograms, 12 of which carried a deliberately wrong suggestion, so it measures accuracy when the machine is wrong rather than review quality overall; very experienced readers fell from 82.3% to 45.5% correct and inexperienced readers from 79.7% to 19.8%. A systematic review of 74 studies found that wrong decision support raised the risk of a wrong decision by 26%, and a second review found automation bias in single tasks with high verification complexity, tied to cognitive load. In audit the direction flips: auditors given contradicting evidence from their firm's AI rather than a human specialist proposed smaller adjustments, while they do anchor on management's explanations. Owhoso's audit-team study shows sequential review catches fewer mechanical errors than independent review, which is consistent with anchoring but does not test it.

What it would take to become a claim: a finance or audit study of preparers or controllers (not auditors) reviewing AI-prepared work, with error rates, or a field report of agreement rates rising while sampled accuracy falls.

- Does showing the agent's confidence reduce anchoring (Goddard lists updated confidence as a mitigator) or deepen it?
