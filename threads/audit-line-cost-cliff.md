# Does the line where a lender or regulator requires an audit instead of a review become a cost cliff once agents close the books below it?

Status: live

Sources: [auditline-jae-badertscher-fees], [auditline-norway-audit-exemption-bunching], [auditline-tn-contractor-licensing], [auditline-sba-sop-50-10-8], [auditline-13cfr-120-826-cdc], [auditline2-7cfr-4279-137-bi-financials], [auditline2-fed-cbem-2040-4-audited-by-commitment], [auditline2-legalclarity-10m-covenant], [auditline2-capital-farm-credit-audit-large-loans], [auditline2-gosba-qoe-in-place-of-audit]

What we know so far: CPA fees more than double for each step in assurance (compilation, review, audit), while reporting-quality proxies are statistically indistinguishable between reviews and audits, and the cost of debt is lower for audits than reviews [auditline-jae-badertscher-fees]; so the review-to-audit step is already a fee doubling that buys lenders pricing comfort, not measured quality. Regulators draw hard size lines: Tennessee requires a reviewed statement for contractor limits up to $3 million and an audited one above, with a surety bond option from 1 July 2026 [auditline-tn-contractor-licensing]; SBA requires audited statements from CDCs with 504 portfolios of $30 million or more and reviewed below [auditline-13cfr-120-826-cdc]; the SBA SOP sets no borrower audit line and leaves review versus audit to the lender's policy for similarly sized loans [auditline-sba-sop-50-10-8].

Counter-case: in Norway's 2011 small-firm audit exemption, no bunching of firms below the revenue threshold appears and only 14% of eligible firm-years forgo the audit [auditline-norway-audit-exemption-bunching], so firms did not shrink to avoid an audit even at an audit cost cited as 6% or more of revenue for small European firms. Firms may not manage size around the line at all; lenders may simply price the difference.

What it would take to become a claim: evidence that agent-prepared closes lower the cost of a review or compilation more than the cost of an audit (a CAS fee survey after 2026, or an audit firm stating no credit for client agents in fee quotes), plus a lender policy or credit agreement setting a review-to-audit size line. With C46 that would support a claim that agents widen the fee gap across the line; the Norway result says the response is pricing, not bunching.

Open sub-questions:
- Do lenders move the line (raise the size at which they demand an audit) when reviews of agent-run books get cheaper, or do they price the gap as the cost-of-debt result suggests?
- Does a surety bond or insurance product, as in Tennessee, replace the audit at the line?

0003F grounder 2 (iter198, 5 auditline2-* sources): no posted bank, credit union, or Farm Credit policy with an audited-vs-reviewed dollar line was found. The Federal Reserve's examination manual asks examiners whether the bank requires audited statements based on the dollar amount of the commitment and to state the dollar minimum, leaving the number to each bank [auditline2-fed-cbem-2040-4-audited-by-commitment]; so the line is a private, per-bank policy choice, which is what a lender could move. USDA's Business and Industry guarantee requires accountant-prepared GAAP statements at minimum and says the agency may require audited statements above $10 million [auditline2-7cfr-4279-137-bi-financials]. A Farm Credit credit officer reserves audits for very large and complex transactions and verifies smaller borrowers by comparing the P&L with tax returns [auditline2-capital-farm-credit-audit-large-loans]. A secondary page calls thresholds around $10 million typical, unsourced [auditline2-legalclarity-10m-covenant]; an SBA lender's marketing says acquisition borrowers rarely have audits and lenders use a quality-of-earnings report instead (vendor) [auditline2-gosba-qoe-in-place-of-audit].
Finding: the review-to-audit line is set bank by bank, not by rule (except USDA's discretionary $10 million), and lenders already substitute tax-return testing and quality-of-earnings reports for audits. That supports the step "lenders move the line or substitute another check" over "owners avoid audits". Still missing for a B claim: a dated change in any lender's line, or a lender accepting agent-measured evidence in place of an audit.

> # By 2036 US banks have replaced the loan size at which they demand an audit with a demand for ledger access and the accounting firm's measured error rate, so the private-company audit shrinks to a product for sales and capital raising and the audit firms' mid-market practices lose their largest source of work
> 
> Year: 2036
> Status: live
> Contradicts: 2036-bank-keeps-the-small-company-ledger. There the bank keeps the small company's books and needs no assurance about how they were made; here the company and its accounting firm keep the books and the bank buys a measurement of how often they are wrong instead of an audit of them.
> Depends on:
> - C45 (the first exception-only agent closes are at unaudited companies whose books a CPA firm keeps on a fixed fee, under a review the owner's monthly acceptance supports): these borrowers already have a firm's agent close and a review, so the error report is a by-product; if firms did not run these closes, the bank would have nothing to read in place of an audit.
> - C46 (the auditor answers the client's agent with its own population agent, so the client's agent earns no audit saving): the audit's price does not fall when the borrower's close is automated, while the review's does, so the gap at each bank's audit line widens; if auditors relied on the client's agent, audits would get cheaper too and banks would keep the line.
> - C19 (the value of agent speed is captured in the lender's monthly package): the bank is the outsider on a monthly clock, so a monthly measured feed is worth more to it than a yearly opinion; if lenders read only annual statements, the audit's timing would not be a weakness.
> Clocks: the Federal Reserve's examination manual already asks whether a bank requires audited statements above a commitment size and leaves the dollar minimum to each bank, so the line can move by board vote without a rule change; USDA's guarantee program may require audits only above $10 million; CPA fees already more than double from review to audit while reporting quality is statistically indistinguishable between the two; bank loan policies are re-approved by boards every year; five-year loans written in 2026 and 2031 refinance in 2031 and 2036.
> Yield: 0.0
> 
> In 2036 a regional bank's commercial loan policy, re-approved each January, no longer says "audited statements required above $10 million of commitments" (illustrative). It says that above that size the borrower must give the bank read access to its ledger and bank feeds and a quarterly error report from its accounting firm, showing blind-sample error rates by task class, or else an audit. Most mid-market borrowers take the first option because it costs a fraction of an audit and gives the bank monthly data. The audit firms' private-company practices shrink to companies being sold or raising outside equity.
> 
> Mechanism: The audit line has always been each bank's choice. As fixed-fee firms move unaudited clients to agent closes (C45), reviews and compilations get cheaper while audits do not, because auditors run their own agents and give no credit for the client's (C46). Borrowers near the line push back at the 2031 refinancings, and banks that already test smaller borrowers against tax returns add a measured alternative: ledger access plus the firm's error report, which also feeds the monthly package the bank cares about (C19). By 2036 most regional banks' policies offer the alternative.
> 
> The CFO of a $60 million borrower (illustrative) stops budgeting for an audit and pays the accounting firm for a quarterly error report. The bank's credit officer reads error-rate trends in the monthly feed instead of a year-old opinion. The audit firm's mid-market partner decides which private clients are worth keeping when audits are needed only for sale and capital raising.
> 
> What would settle it: a bank loan policy or credit agreement offering ledger access or an error report as an alternative to audited statements; a CPA fee survey showing review fees falling while audit fees hold.
> 
> Signposts: by the 2031 refinancing vintage a lender advertises an audit alternative based on ledger access; failing signpost, private-company audit counts hold or rise through 2034.
