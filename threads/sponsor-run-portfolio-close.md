# Will private equity sponsors run one agent close across their portfolio companies?

Status: live
Opened: session 0002F
Yield: 0.60
Touches: C27, C29, C33, C34, C17; scenarios/2031-sponsor-runs-one-close-for-the-portfolio, threads/cas-firm-independence, threads/outside-finance-close-owners
Sources: [pecpa-grant-thornton-new-mountain-close], [pecpa-aicpa-peec-aps-exposure-draft-release], [pefin-ey-pe-shared-services-platforms], [pefin-consero-pe-outsourced-finance], [pefin-crosscountry-rotunda-canter-netsuite], [pefin-bde-tsa-guide], [pefin-ilpa-reporting-template-2025]

What we know so far: a large private equity firm bought into a top-ten US accounting firm in 2024 (threads/cas-firm-independence), and sponsors run portfolio operations teams; the ILPA reporting template revision (recommended from Q1 2026, not yet in sources) asks sponsors for consistent portfolio reporting. No source yet shows a sponsor running portfolio companies' closes from one platform or orchestrator.

What it would take to become a claim: a sponsor or its portfolio operations group describing a shared finance function or single close platform across portfolio companies; a credit agreement clause on sponsor-prepared financials; the C34 independence rule applied to a sponsor-affiliated accounting firm reviewing a portfolio company.

Open sub-questions:
- Do lenders treat a sponsor-prepared close as the borrower's own, and does any covenant say so?
- At exit, do buyers discount companies with no finance function of their own?

0003F draft scenario (iter177, 12 gross / 9.61 net, below 2036 floor 10.47 audit-committee): By 2036 a sponsor's portfolio close is an asset that changes hands at exit; the buyer gets the agent register, settings, named owners, and 60 months of blind-sample error rates in the data room, buys an 18-month close transition agreement, and cuts the price where a model retirement restarted a class's error history. Anchors C33, C38, C17. Contradicts 2036-controller-of-record. Judge: coh 1 mec 1 — the leap that sponsors run portfolio closes at all is unsupported. What would lift it: the same premise this thread already lacks, a sponsor describing a shared close platform across portfolio companies.

0003F grounder (iter179, 5 pefin-* sources): EY describes sponsor shared services for portfolio companies, with cross-charging so PE firms "offer specialized services to their portfolio companies", and projects 50-60% labour arbitrage savings over five years (secondary, projections) [pefin-ey-pe-shared-services-platforms]. Consero says more than 150 PE and VC firms run portfolio companies on its outsourced finance function, with 5-10 day closes (vendor, one tenant per company) [pefin-consero-pe-outsourced-finance]. A Rotunda Capital platform company consolidated four QuickBooks instances into one NetSuite environment, six entities on one platform (vendor case; inside one platform company, not across the portfolio) [pefin-crosscountry-rotunda-canter-netsuite]. Transition services agreements at carve-outs typically run 6 to 24 months and finance and accounting coverage includes month-end close support; entangled single-ERP separations run 18 to 36 months [pefin-bde-tsa-guide]. ILPA's 2025 reporting template applies from Q1 2026 at fund level and says nothing about portfolio closes [pefin-ilpa-reporting-template-2025].
Finding: the exit step of the 2036 draft (the seller keeps closing the books under a services agreement) already exists as TSA practice for carve-outs; the missing premise is still a sponsor running closes across unrelated portfolio companies (only outsourced vendors and projections). Counter: standardisation runs through vendors, one tenant per company, so each company keeps its own books and could leave with them.
What it would take now: a sponsor-owned shared finance service closing books for several unrelated portfolio companies (named sponsor, count of companies).

> # By 2036 a private equity sponsor's portfolio close is an asset that changes hands at exit, because its portfolio companies have no finance function of their own left to take it over, so buyers price the close configuration and its error history the way they price customer contracts
> 
> Year: 2036
> Status: live
> Contradicts: 2036-controller-of-record. There a company too small for its own controller rents a licensed signature and still owns its close; here a sponsor-owned company owns neither, because the sponsor's shared finance service ran its close for five years, and at sale the close is transferred under a services agreement or rebuilt at a discount.
> Depends on:
> - C33 (agents sign the preparer's checklist lines and each agent's named owner sub-certifies its settings and measured results): the named owners sit at the sponsor's shared service, so what transfers at exit is a register of agents, settings, and owners; if people still signed for the numbers item by item, the close would leave with them and nothing could be sold.
> - C38 (a provider's model retirement is a program change at every customer at once and the blind sample restarts): a buyer can rely on the close's error history only for the model versions it covers, so a close that just crossed a retirement sells at a discount; if model changes did not restart the evidence, the history would carry over and timing would not matter.
> - C53 (the junior seat disappears on both sides of the audit, and with it the people who could prepare the close): the buyer cannot staff a finance function at closing, which is why it buys the sponsor's close instead of rebuilding; if juniors were still being trained, a buyer would hire a team and let the sponsor's close lapse.
> Clocks: sponsors already run shared services for portfolio companies and charge them for it, and one outsourced finance provider says more than 150 private equity and venture firms run portfolio companies on its service; transition services agreements at carve-outs already cover month-end close support for six to twenty-four months; the usual five-to-seven-year hold means companies bought in 2029 to 2031 are sold between 2034 and 2037.
> Yield: 0.0
> 
> In 2036 a strategic buyer acquiring a $200 million (illustrative) industrial distributor from a sponsor finds the close in the data room. The company has a CFO and two people who answer agents' questions; its entries, reconciliations, and covenant package have come from the sponsor's shared finance service for five years. The data room holds the agent register, each agent's settings and named owner, and sixty months of blind-sample error rates by task class. The purchase agreement includes an eighteen-month (illustrative) close transition agreement under which the sponsor's service keeps closing the books while the buyer moves the configuration onto its own platform, and the price was cut because one class's error history restarts after a model retirement in the final year.
> 
> Mechanism: Sponsors already sell shared services to their portfolio companies, and outsourced finance providers already close books for many sponsors' companies. Once agents do most of that close, the sponsor's service runs dozens of companies' closes on one platform, and each agent's named owner sits at the sponsor (C33). At exit the buyer cannot hire a finance team in time (C53), so the close transfers the way carve-out finance already does, under a transition agreement covering month-end close, and diligence prices the error history, which counts only for the model versions it covers (C38). Quality-of-earnings providers add a close section, and sponsors time exits around model retirements.
> 
> The buyer's corporate controller stops treating finance integration as hiring and starts migrating an agent register. The sponsor's operating partner sets the exit date partly by the model calendar. The diligence provider reports on the error history as it reports on revenue quality.
> 
> What would settle it: a sponsor's shared finance service closing books for several unrelated portfolio companies; a purchase or transition agreement covering an agent-run close.
> 
> Signposts: by 2030 a large sponsor announces a portfolio-wide close service; failing signpost, by 2034 sponsor exits still transfer companies with their own finance teams and no close transition terms.

> # By 2031 a private equity sponsor buys one agent close for all its portfolio companies from a single outsourced finance provider, so a portfolio company's finance team shrinks to the people who answer the agents, and lenders must decide whether a close the sponsor procures is still the borrower's own
> 
> Year: 2031
> Status: live
> Contradicts: 2031-the-accounting-firm-runs-the-small-company-close. There each owner's own CPA firm runs the close and the owner's monthly sitting is the control; here the owner is a sponsor with forty companies, the close is bought centrally from one provider, and the person who accepts each company's agent decisions sits at the sponsor, not the company.
> Depends on:
> - C45 (a firm whose agents post a client's entries can give the lender a review only while the client's owner accepts the agent's decisions, because posting is a management responsibility): here the owner who accepts is the sponsor's portfolio finance lead, so the review firm must be one the sponsor does not control; if posting did not end independence, the provider could also review, and lenders would see one firm doing everything.
> - C33 (agents sign the preparer's checklist lines and each agent's named owner sub-certifies its settings and results): the named owners of forty companies' agents sit in one sponsor team, which is what lets one team run forty closes; if people signed for numbers item by item, the work could not be pooled.
> - C19 (the value of agent speed is captured in the lender's monthly package): the sponsor buys the shared close to deliver forty lender packages on one calendar; if lenders read only annual statements, there would be no monthly deadline to pool.
> Clocks: one outsourced finance provider says more than 150 private equity and venture firms already run portfolio companies on its service, with five-to-ten-day closes; sponsors already sell shared services to portfolio companies and charge them for it; ILPA's reporting template applies to funds in their investment period from the first quarter of 2026; credit agreements signed in 2026 on five-year terms refinance in 2031, when lenders can write terms on sponsor-procured closes.
> Yield: 0.0
> 
> In 2031 a sponsor with forty (illustrative) portfolio companies has one close contract. Its provider's agents post every company's entries, and the sponsor's portfolio finance lead accepts each company's agent decisions in a weekly queue sorted by company. Each company keeps a CFO and one or two people who answer the agents' questions. At the 2031 refinancings, lenders add a clause that the borrower's statements must be reviewed by a firm neither the sponsor nor the provider controls, because the person accepting the books works for the owner rather than the company.
> 
> Mechanism: Sponsors already put portfolio companies on one outsourced finance provider and charge them for shared services. As agents take the preparation, the provider runs forty closes on one platform and the agents' named owners pool in the sponsor's team (C33). The sponsor gains forty lender packages on one calendar (C19). The independence rule then forces a split (C45): the provider posts, the sponsor accepts, and a third firm reviews, which lenders write into the 2031 refinancings.
> 
> The portfolio company CFO stops managing a finance team and answers for one company inside the sponsor's queue. The sponsor's portfolio finance lead accepts decisions for forty companies. The lender's credit officer asks who accepted the books and who reviewed them.
> 
> What would settle it: a sponsor contracting one close provider across its portfolio; a credit agreement naming the reviewer's independence from the sponsor.
> 
> Signposts: by 2029 a large sponsor announces a portfolio-wide close contract; failing signpost, by the 2031 refinancings portfolio companies still each run their own close.
